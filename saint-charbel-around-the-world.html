From Annaya to Every Continent

Saint Charbel Around the World

Saint Charbel never left the mountains of Lebanon. Yet a century after his death, candles burn for him from Phoenix to Lviv, from Quebec City to Sicily, and whole streets close down when his relics pass. This is the story of how a silent hermit became one of the most widely loved saints on earth - and where you can find his shrines beyond Lebanon.

How a Hermit Traveled the World

Two currents carried him. The first was the Lebanese emigration: families who left Mount Lebanon for the Americas, Australia, Europe, and West Africa packed their devotion with them, built Maronite parishes, and put his image in their new churches. The second was his canonization in 1977 - the first Lebanese saint of the modern era - which placed him on the universal Church's calendar and in front of Catholics who had never met a Lebanese emigrant.

The result, as the Catholic Near East Welfare Association describes it, is a devotion that has entered the mainstream of Christian life far beyond the Maronite Church: his hooded, downcast image glows before rows of tapers "from Phoenix to Lviv, Ukraine, from Quebec City to Cefalu, Sicily, in churches Catholic and Orthodox." And the flow runs both ways - more than four million visitors a year now climb to his tomb at Annaya, Christians, Muslims, and Druze among them.

Mexico: The Ribbon Tradition

Nowhere outside Lebanon has Saint Charbel taken root more deeply than in Mexico - and not only among Lebanese-Mexican families. The best-loved Mexican custom began, according to the Archdiocese of Mexico City's weekly Desde la Fe, at the Maronite Cathedral of Our Lady of Balvanera in the city's historic center. More than twenty years ago, a woman who had prayed to every saint she knew stopped before the unfamiliar image of Saint Charbel and, finding no paper, wrote her petition on a scrap of ribbon and tied it to the statue. When she returned days later to say her prayer had been answered, the rector, Monsignor Rogelio Peralta, suggested she give thanks the same way she had asked - with a ribbon.

The listones - ribbons inscribed with petitions and thanks - spread from Balvanera across Mexico and into other countries, and today they stream from images of San Charbel in churches across the country. Mexico City also has a cathedral bearing his name, the Catedral San Charbel in Santa Fe, keeping his prayer and story at the center of a thriving Maronite community.

United States: A Shrine That Never Closes

In Phoenix, Arizona, St. Joseph Maronite Church is home to the Shrine of St. Sharbel, dedicated on March 26, 2017 by Bishop Elias Zaidan of the Eparchy of Our Lady of Lebanon of Los Angeles. The parish describes it as a pilgrimage destination for the city - and it is open 365 days a year, 24 hours a day, so the night shift can pray as easily as the Sunday crowd. The parish had already hosted the saint's traveling relics in 2016, drawing the faithful from across the region.

In North Jackson, Ohio, the Basilica and National Shrine of Our Lady of Lebanon has been the Maronite heart of pilgrimage in America since the 1960s - eighty acres bought in 1961 after a novena for the land, and a shrine raised as a stone-by-stone homage to Harissa. It is to the United States what Harissa is to Lebanon, and it anchors the same devotion to the country's saint.

Australia: Ten Thousand in the Streets

On May 8, 2024 - the 196th anniversary of Saint Charbel's birth - the Sydney suburb of Punchbowl effectively shut down. More than ten thousand people filled the streets to welcome a replica of Saint Charbel's tomb, carried by seventy white-clad pallbearers and bearing a first-class relic, to its permanent home at Saint Charbel's Monastery and Parish, served by the Lebanese Maronite Order. Neighbors lit candles on their front lawns as the procession passed. EWTN reported it as the installation of the second shrine in the world dedicated to the saint - a piece of Annaya in New South Wales, for everyone who cannot make the pilgrimage to Lebanon.

And Everywhere Else

Beyond these anchor points, the devotion is genuinely global: Maronite cathedrals and parishes across Latin America, Europe, and Africa; his image in Orthodox as well as Catholic churches; pilgrim groups that fly to Annaya from continents away for the 22nd of the month and the July feast. Pope Leo XIV named exactly this when he stood at the tomb in December 2025: "thousands of pilgrims from different countries come here to spend a day in prayer."

The hermit who sought no one now has seekers everywhere. If you are far from Lebanon, the devotion needs no airfare: the novena, a simple prayer for healing, or a quiet Rosary prayed with his daily meditation, or the 2026 Charbel film that carried his story to cinema screens put you in the same line of prayer as the crowds at the tomb.
