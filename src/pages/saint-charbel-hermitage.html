Saint Charbel's Hermitage: Twenty-Three Years Above Annaya

A fifteen-minute climb above the monastery of Annaya stands a cluster of narrow cells around a small chapel - the Hermitage of Saints Peter and Paul, where Saint Charbel lived from 1875 until his death. Here is what the hermitage is, how he lived there, and what you see when you visit.

A Retreat Older Than the Monastery

The hermitage predates the monastery below it. In 1798 two seekers, Yusuf Abi Ramia and Daoud Moussa Khalifeh, bought the hilltop plot and built a chapel and cells there to withdraw from the world. The chapel was first dedicated to the Transfiguration; in 1814, at the request of Patriarch John Helou, it was rededicated to the Apostles Peter and Paul, and the hermitage passed to the Lebanese Maronite Order. When the present monastery church was finished in 1828, the hermitage became its annex - the place, in the old Maronite monastic pattern, where a monk could go to be alone with God. Before Charbel, four hermits had lived there, the first of them Father Elisha al-Hardini, brother of Saint Nimatullah al-Hardini.

February 15, 1875: Permission to Withdraw

Charbel had spent sixteen years in the monastery below - novice, student, priest, the brother who did the hardest fieldwork - asking all along for the hermit's life. The tradition records that his superiors hesitated until the incident of the lamp: his lamp, found full of water instead of oil, gave light anyway, and they read it as a sign that his request came from God. On February 15, 1875, he climbed the hill for good. He was forty-six.

How He Lived There

For twenty-three years his days barely varied: prayer from midnight until his Mass at nine in the morning, kneeling on the floor; fieldwork on the mountain terraces from April to September; one meal a day; a cell so narrow a tall man could not stretch out, with a mat of goat hair for a bed and a log of wood for a pillow. Snow blocked the paths in winter; he did not come down. The monks who kept vigil the night he died asked one another how a man had lived twenty-three winters like that - and answered themselves: truly, this hermit was a saint. He died at the hermitage on Christmas Eve, 1898, and villagers carried his body down through the snow to the monastery cemetery - the story continues on the tomb page.

Visiting the Hermitage Today

The hermitage still stands above the monastery, about a fifteen-minute uphill walk, at roughly a thousand meters above sea level with views to the sea. Pilgrims visit his cell, the chapel where he prayed, and the objects the hermits left behind - tools blackened by winter fires, the worn holy pictures, the great oak outside. It is part of every Annaya pilgrimage: the full route, drive times, and what to see in what order are on our pilgrim's guide to Annaya. Frequently Asked Questions

Where is Saint Charbel's hermitage?

Above the Monastery of Saint Maron at Annaya, in the Batroun district of North Lebanon - about a fifteen-minute uphill walk from the monastery, at roughly 1,000 meters above sea level.

How long did Saint Charbel live in the hermitage?

Twenty-three years. He received permission and moved up on February 15, 1875, and died there on December 24, 1898.

What is the Hermitage of Saints Peter and Paul?

A small chapel and cells built in 1798 by two lay hermits, rededicated to Saints Peter and Paul in 1814, and later annexed to the monastery below as its house of withdrawal. Saint Charbel was the fifth hermit to live there.

Can you visit Saint Charbel's cell?

Yes. The hermitage is open to pilgrims as part of a visit to Annaya: his cell, the chapel, and the simple objects of his life are preserved. See the pilgrim's guide for the full route.

What was daily life like in the hermitage?

Prayer from midnight until Mass at nine, farm work on the mountain terraces, one meal a day, a mat and a wooden log for a bed - for twenty-three years, through snow and summer heat, without complaint, according to the monks who knew him.
