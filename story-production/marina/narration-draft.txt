In the early centuries of Lebanese monastic life, in the Qalamoun hills near Tripoli, there lived, in the Lebanese tradition, a girl whose mother had died when she was small. The old Lebanese story tells us her father was named Eugenius, and that she loved him very much. This is the story of Marina, whom Maronites know as Marina the Monk, remembered in the Qadisha Valley. Her story comes down to us as tradition, passed on for a very long time.

PAGE 2
Eugenius was a devout man. He wanted to give what he owned to the poor and become a monk at the Monastery of Qannoubine, high in the Holy Valley. A monastery is a home where people live and pray together. Marina listened as her father told her.

PAGE 3
Women could not live in that monastery. Eugenius was ready to go. Marina found her courage and asked him a hard question. "Will you save your own soul, and destroy mine?" The tradition remembers her question to this day. She did not want to be left behind. She wanted to belong to God and to stay near her father.

PAGE 4
So she cut her hair, put on a monk's clothes, and took the name Marinos. Father and daughter walked into Qannoubine together. The monks welcomed the young brother. For years, the two prayed side by side, until Eugenius died.

PAGE 5
Years passed. One day, Marinos was sent with other monks on monastery business. They stayed overnight at an inn. Soon after, a baby was born in that house, and someone told a lie. They said the baby belonged to the young monk. Marinos was brought before the abbot, the leader of the monastery. She did not defend herself. The abbot sent her away.

PAGE 6
Marinos lived outside the monastery, at its gate. The innkeeper left the baby there too. The old story says shepherds brought milk, and Marinos fed the child. For years, she cared for the boy and prayed every day. Many people thought badly of her. She kept caring, and she kept praying.

PAGE 7
At last, the monks went to the abbot and asked him to let Brother Marinos come back. He agreed. She returned to the hardest jobs in the house: cooking, cleaning, and carrying water. The boy came with her. Marinos did the work quietly and kept her faith.

PAGE 8
Later, Marinos became ill and died. The monks prepared her body for burial. Then they learned the truth. Their brother was a woman, and the story people had believed was false. The monks wept. They had judged her wrongly, and now they understood. The old story adds that a monk who was blind in one eye touched her body and could see again. Christians tell this as a sign of God's mercy, not a promise for every illness.

PAGE 9
They buried her in a grotto near the monastery, the place Maronite tradition honors as her tomb. Long ago, her remains were carried far away to Venice, where people honored her for centuries. In July 2018, around her feast day, her relics visited Lebanon on a pilgrimage. Pilgrims still climb down to her little chapel beside Qannoubine. Marina's story reminds us that God sees the heart, even when people do not.

PAGE 10
Here is something to wonder about. Marina was blamed for something she did not do. How do you think that felt? Would you like to read a verse together with a parent or another trusted adult? It is in the First Book of Samuel, chapter sixteen, verse seven. It says that people look at appearances, but the Lord looks at the heart. This week, try one small act. If you see someone blamed unfairly, you can be kind to them. You can tell the truth, and you can tell a trusted grown-up. A short prayer: Dear God, you see my heart. Help me to be truthful and kind. Saint Marina, pray for us. Amen.
