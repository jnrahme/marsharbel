Therese of Lisieux: A Little Way of Love

In the French town of Alencon, a girl named Therese Martin was born in 1873. Her parents were Louis and Zelie, and she grew up among sisters. When Therese was four, her mother died. The family moved to Lisieux. Therese's family kept caring for her as she grew.

At home in Lisieux, Therese learned to pray with her family. Her older sisters Pauline and Marie later entered a Carmelite convent. There, sisters lived together, prayed, worked, and served God in a quiet community. Therese hoped to join them. But she was still young, and she was asked to wait. Her hope would lead her much farther than the streets of Lisieux before she could return to begin that life.

In 1887, fourteen-year-old Therese traveled with her family on a pilgrimage to Rome. A pilgrimage is a journey made for prayer. With other pilgrims, she met Pope Leo the Thirteenth. She asked him if she could enter Carmel at fifteen. After the visit, she returned home and continued seeking permission. Her family and the Church helped guide the decision. The wait was not over yet, but she had spoken about the life she hoped to live.

The following year, permission came. In 1888, at fifteen, Therese entered the Carmel of Lisieux. The sisters shared prayer, chores, and the work of living together. Therese's days would unfold within this small community.

Life in a community was not always easy. People needed patience with one another, even while doing ordinary work. Therese called her path the little way: trusting God's mercy and practicing love in daily life. She did not measure love by how grand a task looked. A small act of help could matter. Love could begin with the person already beside her.

Later, Therese's father became seriously ill. He died in 1894. She loved him deeply, and this was another loss in her family. At Carmel, her life of prayer and work continued through grief. She was also asked to guide newer sisters, called novices, as they began religious life. She taught them the little way she was learning herself. Her quiet community held both sorrow and people who still needed her care.

Therese also cared about people far beyond Lisieux. She prayed for missionaries and wrote to two priests serving in mission work. Though she remained at Carmel, her letters traveled where she could not. She read Saint Paul's teaching about love and found a way to understand her place in the Church. Different people had different work to do. Love belonged at the heart of it all. Her prayers and letters became part of that shared work.

As the years passed, Therese wrote memories and reflections that became her autobiographical manuscripts. They told of her family, her life at Carmel, and her trust in God's mercy. Later, she became very ill. The sisters cared for her, and she moved to the infirmary, the convent's place for sick people. She continued writing during her final illness.

Therese died in 1897, at twenty-four. Her manuscripts reached readers who had never visited Lisieux. The Church named her a saint and later a Doctor of the Church. That title means her teaching helps others grow in faith. The little way she practiced among a few sisters was now being read in many places. Her life had been short and her home small. Yet her invitation to trust God and love the person nearby kept traveling.

Therese hoped to serve God, but much of her life stayed within one quiet community. What helped her care for people beyond its walls? You can talk about it with someone who cares for you.

Read John, chapter fifteen, verse twelve together. In this verse, Jesus asks his followers to love one another as he has loved them.

Try one small act of care today. Help with a chore, or listen to someone at home.

God, help me trust your love. Teach me to notice the person beside me and care for them with patience. Saint Therese, pray for us. Amen.
